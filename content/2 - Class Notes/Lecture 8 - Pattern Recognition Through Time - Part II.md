
> [!abstract] **Core Idea**  
> **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)** asks:
> 
> > _"How similar are these two sequences?"_
> 
> **Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)** ask a different question:
> 
> > _"Could this sequence have been generated by this process?"_
> 
> Instead of storing or comparing individual examples, an HMM learns a **probabilistic model** of an entire class of sequences.

---

# Why Do We Need Something Beyond DTW?

In the previous section, DTW solved an important problem:

- different speaking speeds
    
- stretched gestures
    
- compressed handwriting
    
- variable-length whistle recordings
    

DTW aligns two sequences before comparing them.

```text
Sequence A  ─────────────┐
                         │  DTW Alignment
Sequence B  ─────────────┘
```

This works extremely well **when we already have a reference example**.

But many recognition tasks require something more general.

Suppose we want to recognize the spoken word

```text
"Hello"
```

Should we store

- every person's pronunciation?
    
- every speaking speed?
    
- every accent?
    
- every recording?
    

Clearly not.

Instead, we would like to learn

> **the statistical structure shared by all valid pronunciations.**

This is exactly what Hidden Markov Models do.

---

# Two Different Ways of Thinking

Instead of thinking of recognition as **matching**, HMMs treat it as **generation**.

```text
DTW

Unknown Sequence
        │
        ▼
Compare with Stored Example
        │
        ▼
Distance
```

versus

```text
HMM

Unknown Sequence
        │
        ▼
"What process could have produced this?"
        │
        ▼
Probability
```

This seemingly small change is extremely powerful.

Instead of memorizing examples, we learn **how examples are produced**.

# From Markov Models to Hidden Markov Models

An HMM builds upon an ordinary **Markov Model**.

A Markov Model consists of

- states
    
- transitions between states
    

```text
State A ───► State B ───► State C
```

The defining assumption is the **First-Order Markov Property**.

---

## First-Order Markov Property

The future depends only on the present.

```text
Past ─────► Current ─────► Future
              ▲
              │
Only this matters.
```

Mathematically,

$$  
P(X_{t+1}\mid X_t,X_{t-1},...,X_1)

P(X_{t+1}\mid X_t)  
$$

The entire history is summarized by the **current state**.

---

> [!tip]  
> Think of the current state as carrying all the information needed to predict the immediate future.

---

# Why Are They "Hidden"?

In a normal Markov Model,

we can directly observe the states.

```text
Sunny → Cloudy → Rainy
```

An HMM changes this assumption.

The states become invisible.

Instead,

each hidden state produces an observation.

```text
Hidden States

S₁ ─────► S₂ ─────► S₃

│          │          │

▼          ▼          ▼

o₁         o₂         o₃
```

The arrows downward represent **emissions**.

We observe

```text
o₁  o₂  o₃
```

but never directly observe

```text
S₁  S₂  S₃
```

One of the main goals of HMM inference is therefore:

> **Infer the most likely hidden state sequence that produced the observations.**

# HMM Structure Through Time

The representation used in most AI textbooks (including Russell & Norvig) is a temporal Bayesian network.

```text
Hidden States

X₁ ─► X₂ ─► X₃ ─► X₄

│      │      │      │

▼      ▼      ▼      ▼

E₁     E₂     E₃     E₄
```

where

- $X_t$ = hidden state at time $t$
    
- $E_t$ = observed evidence at time $t$
    

Notice the repeated pattern. Each state influences

1. the next hidden state
    
2. the observation emitted at that time
    

This repeated structure makes HMMs especially suitable for temporal data.

# Key Takeaways

- DTW compares **two sequences**, while HMMs model **an entire family of sequences**.
    
- HMMs assume an underlying process evolves through hidden states that cannot be observed directly.
    
- We observe **emissions**, not the states themselves.
    
- The First-Order Markov Property assumes the future depends only on the current state.
    
- Recognition becomes an inference problem:
    
    > **Which hidden state sequence most likely generated these observations?**
    
- This generative viewpoint makes HMMs one of the foundational models for temporal pattern recognition.

# Hidden Markov Models in Practice — Sign Language Recognition

> [!abstract]
> The previous section explained **how to construct** a Hidden Markov Model.
>
> This section answers a different question:
>
> **Why are HMMs so effective for recognizing gestures, even when the available features are weak?**
>
> The key insight is that an HMM learns **both the observations and their temporal organization**. It models:
>
> - what is likely to happen,
> - the order in which it happens,
> - how long each stage usually lasts,
> - and how much variation each stage allows.


## Why Sign Language is an Ideal HMM Problem

Unlike image classification, sign language recognition is fundamentally a **temporal recognition** problem.

A single frame contains only the current hand position.

It does **not** tell us:

- where the hand came from,
- where it is going,
- or which gesture is being performed.

For example, consider three frames.

```text
Frame 1        Frame 2        Frame 3

   ○              ○              ○
  /|             /|             /|
  /             /             /
```

Looking at a single frame, the hand could be

- moving upward,
- moving downward,
- or simply paused.

Only the **entire sequence** reveals the gesture.

```text
Raise Hand
     │
     ▼
 Pause
     │
     ▼
Lower Hand

↓

Gesture = "I"
```

> [!important]
> A gesture is **not a single image**.
>
> It is a **process unfolding through time**.
>
> Hidden Markov Models are specifically designed to model these kinds of sequential processes.


## Static Recognition vs Sequential Recognition

Traditional machine learning answers:

> **"What am I looking at right now?"**

Hidden Markov Models answer:

> **"What sequence of events most likely produced these observations?"**

| Static Recognition | Sequential Recognition (HMM) |
|--------------------|------------------------------|
| Independent observations | Ordered observations |
| Single frame | Entire sequence |
| Ignores temporal evolution | Learns temporal evolution |
| Best for images | Best for gestures, speech, handwriting |

The goal is **not** to build the easiest classifier.

If perfect features were used,

Instead, the lecture demonstrates that

> Even weak observations can become highly informative when combined with **temporal structure**.

The HMM succeeds because it learns **how observations evolve over time**, not just their values.

## Hidden States Represent Motion Phases

The HMM does **not** memorize the entire gesture.

Instead, it divides the gesture into meaningful phases.

```text
Gesture

   │

   │

```

Each phase becomes a hidden state.

```text
State 1
   │

   │
   ▼
State 3
```

Each hidden state learns:

- expected observations
- expected duration
- likely next state

## A Hidden State is NOT a Single Frame

A very common misunderstanding is

> One state = One frame

This is **not** how HMMs work.

Instead,

one hidden state usually represents **many consecutive frames**.

```text
Video Frames

□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□

↓

Hidden States

111111222233333
```

Many observations may belong to the same hidden state.

This is precisely why **self-transitions** exist.

## Why Self-Transitions Matter

Imagine two people performing the same gesture.

### Person A

```text

Pause

Lower
```

### Person B

```text
Raise

Looooooooong Pause

Lower
```

Both gestures contain almost identical observations.

The primary difference is **how long the pause lasts**.

Without self-transitions,

both gestures would appear nearly identical.

With self-transitions,

the HMM naturally models

```text
Stay...

Stay...

Still here...

Now continue.
```

instead of

```text
Move immediately.
```

## How the HMM Encodes This Difference

The lecture modifies two parameters.

## 1. Transition Probability

Gesture **"I"**

```text

↺ 0.50
```

Gesture **"We"**

```text
Pause

↺ 0.80
```

A larger self-transition probability means the model expects to remain in that state longer.

Using the expected duration relationship,

$$
\text{Expected Duration}
=
\frac{1}{1-p_{\text{self}}}
$$

where

- $p_{\text{self}}$ = self-transition probability.

Examples

| Self Transition | Expected Frames |
|---------------:|----------------:|
| 0.50 | 2 |
| 0.80 | 5 |
| 0.90 | 10 |

The "We" gesture also exhibits greater variation.

### Wide Gaussian

```text

```

A wider Gaussian represents greater uncertainty about the observations while remaining in that state.

## Timing Becomes a Learned Feature

Traditional classifiers depend almost entirely on input features.

For example

- Δx
- Δy
- velocity
- angle

An HMM learns something extra.

It automatically captures

- expected order
- expected duration
- expected variability
- probable transitions

These temporal characteristics become **features of the model itself**.

> [!insight]
> Two gestures may have nearly identical observations but completely different timing.
>
> The HMM learns this timing automatically.

> Up to this point, we have learned **how an HMM represents a sequential process** using hidden states, transition probabilities, and emission probabilities.
>

>
> > **Given an observation sequence, how do we determine the hidden state sequence that most likely produced it?**
>
> This problem is called **decoding**, and the standard solution is the **Viterbi Algorithm**.


## The Three Fundamental HMM Problems

One of the easiest ways to understand Hidden Markov Models is to realize that they solve **three different problems**, each requiring a different algorithm.

```text
                    Hidden Markov Models
                           │
        ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
        │                                     │                                     │
        ▼                                     ▼                                     ▼
  Evaluate Sequence           Decode States           Learn Parameters
   "How likely?"                     "Which path?"            "Improve model?"
        │                                     │                                     │
    Forward                             Viterbi                   Baum–Welch (EM)
```

Each algorithm answers a different question.

| Problem | Question | Algorithm |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| Evaluation | How likely is this observation sequence? | Forward Algorithm |
| Decoding | Which hidden states most likely generated it? | **Viterbi Algorithm** |
| Learning | How should the HMM parameters be updated? | Baum–Welch |

> [!important]
> These algorithms work **together**, not independently.
>
> - **Forward** evaluates a sequence.
> - **Viterbi** explains a sequence.
> - **Baum–Welch** improves the model from many sequences.


## The Decoding Problem

Suppose we observe only the outputs.

```text
Observations

+2   +1    0   -2   -3
```

We never observe the hidden states.

```text
???

???

???

???

???
```

Instead, we ask

> **Which hidden state sequence most likely generated these observations?**

This is the **decoding problem**.

## GPS Analogy

A useful way to think about Viterbi is through GPS navigation.

Imagine driving through a city.

Your GPS records noisy location measurements.

```text
GPS Measurements

●     ●      ●      ●      ●
```

The actual roads are hidden.

```text
Road Network

Highway

Residential

Highway

Highway
```

Your task is

> **Given these noisy observations, what road did I most likely travel?**

This is exactly what Viterbi does.

It reconstructs the **most probable hidden path** through the HMM.

## Observations vs Hidden States

An HMM always contains two parallel sequences.

```text
Hidden States

S₁ → S₂ → S₂ → S₃ → S₄

 │     │     │     │     │

 ▼     ▼     ▼     ▼     ▼

Observations

o₁    o₂    o₃    o₄    o₅
```

The observations are known.

The states are hidden.

The Viterbi Algorithm attempts to recover

```text
?

↓

S₁

↓

S₂

↓

S₂

↓

S₃
```


## The Viterbi Trellis

Instead of exploring every possible path independently, Viterbi organizes the computation into a **trellis**.

Each

- **column** represents one observation (time step),
- **row** represents one hidden state.

```text
                 Time →

          o₁      o₂      o₃      o₄

S₁        ●──────●──────●──────●

          │╲     │╲

S₂        ●─╲────●─╲────●──────●

            ╲│      ╲│

S₃          ●──────●──────●────●
```

Every path through this graph represents one possible explanation of the observations.

# What Determines a Good Path?

Each path accumulates probability from **two independent sources**.

## 1. Transition Probability

How likely is it to move between hidden states?

```text
S₁

0.8 ↺

0.2 ↓

S₂
```

These probabilities describe the **temporal behavior** of the process.

## 2. Emission Probability

How likely is a hidden state to produce the observed value?

Suppose the observation is

$$
\Delta y = 2
$$

Every hidden state asks

> "How likely am I to generate this observation?"

```text
Observation = +2

          State 1

          █████████

          State 2

          ███

          State 3

          █
```

The observation contributes to every state—but with different probabilities.

##  Computing a Path Probability

Every step along a path combines

- the probability of reaching the previous state,
- the transition probability,
- the emission probability.

The recurrence is

$$
\delta_t(j)
=
\max_i
\left[
\delta_{t-1}(i)
\cdot
a_{ij}
\right]
\cdot
b_j(o_t)
$$

where

- $\delta_t(j)$ = best probability of reaching state $j$ at time $t$
- $a_{ij}$ = transition probability
- $b_j(o_t)$ = emission probability for observation $o_t$

---

### Simple Example

Suppose

Previous path probability

$$
1
$$

Transition probability

$$
0.8
$$

Emission probability

$$
0.5
$$

Overall

$$
1
\times
0.8
\times
0.5
=
0.4
$$

Every possible path is evaluated similarly.

## Why a Greedy Strategy Fails

A tempting idea is

> "Always choose the locally largest transition."

Unfortunately,

this can produce a poor overall path.

```text
Largest Immediate Choice

↓

Good now

↓

Terrible later
```

A locally optimal decision does **not** guarantee a globally optimal sequence.

## Why Dynamic Programming Works

Imagine there are **millions** of possible hidden paths.

Do we need to store all of them?

No.

Suppose two different paths arrive at

```text
(State 2, Time 5)
```

Path A

```text
Probability = 0.02
```

Path B

```text
Probability = 0.15
```

Both continue through **exactly the same future**.

Since Path B is already better,

Path A can **never** overtake it later.

Therefore

```text
Discard Path A

Keep Path B
```

This is the key optimization.

> [!important]
> Viterbi never stores every path.
>
> It stores **only the best path reaching each state at each time step**.

This is why the algorithm is a **Dynamic Programming** algorithm rather than an exhaustive search.

## Dynamic Programming Intuition

Instead of

```text
Millions of complete paths
```

Viterbi keeps

```text
Best path

↓

to every state

↓

at every time
```

```text
Time 1

State A

↓

Best Path

────────────────────

Time 2

State B

↓

Best Path

────────────────────

Time 3

State C

↓

Best Path
```

The optimal solution is built from these optimal partial solutions.

## Numerical Underflow

Because probabilities are multiplied repeatedly,

they quickly become extremely small.

Example

$$
0.5
\times
0.2
\times
10^{-7}
\times
0.4
\times
0.3
\times
\cdots
$$

After a long sequence,

floating-point precision is lost.


## The Solution: Log Probabilities

Instead of multiplying probabilities,

implementations compute

$$
\log(ab)
=
\log a
+
\log b
$$

Products become sums.

```text
Probability Space

×

×

×

×

↓

Very Small Numbers

────────────────────

Log Space

+

+

+

+

↓

Numerically Stable
```

> [!tip]
> Almost every practical HMM implementation performs computations in **log space** to avoid numerical underflow.


## Choosing Between Multiple HMMs

Suppose two gesture models exist.

```text
Gesture "I"

Gesture "We"
```

Both evaluate the same observation sequence.

| Model | Log Probability |
|------|----------------:|
| I | -7.96 |
| We | -9.43 |

The larger probability (or less negative log probability) wins.

```text
Observation Sequence

↓

Evaluate HMM(I)

↓

Evaluate HMM(We)

↓

Choose Higher Likelihood
```

Recognition therefore becomes a **model comparison** problem.

## What Makes Viterbi Powerful?

Viterbi does **not** classify observations independently.

Instead, it accumulates evidence over the entire sequence.

It simultaneously considers

- expected observations,
- state transitions,
- expected durations,
- temporal order.

Even weak observations become informative when interpreted within the context of an entire sequence.

> [!success]
> Dynamic Time Warping finds the **best alignment** between two sequences.
>
> The Viterbi Algorithm finds the **most probable hidden explanation** for a sequence under a probabilistic model.
>
> This transition—from matching sequences to inferring hidden processes—is one of the major conceptual advances introduced by Hidden Markov Models.

### Emission Probabilities

These answer

> **What observations are expected while the system is in each state?**

```text
State 1

Mostly

Positive Δy

──────────────

State 2

Mostly

Around Zero

──────────────

State 3

Mostly

Negative Δy
```

Training therefore learns

- **how the process evolves**, and
- **what each stage looks like**.

---

## Step 1 — Initial Segmentation

At the beginning,

the model has no idea where one hidden state ends and another begins.

The lecture therefore starts with a simple approximation.

Divide every training sequence into equal pieces.

```text
Observation Sequence

□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□□

↓

Initial Guess

|--------|--------|--------|

State 1   State 2   State 3
```

This segmentation is almost certainly wrong,

but it provides a starting point.

> [!note]
> Machine learning algorithms often begin with a rough initial guess and then improve it iteratively.
>
> HMM training follows exactly this philosophy.

---

# Estimating Transition Probabilities

Suppose the initial segmentation assigns

```text
State 1

□□□□
```

Four observations belong to State 1.

The model estimates

- expected duration,
- probability of remaining,
- probability of moving forward.

If the expected duration is

$$
4
$$

then

$$
P(\text{leave})
=
\frac{1}{4}
=
0.25
$$

and

$$
P(\text{stay})
=
0.75
$$

Intuitively,

the longer a state typically lasts,

the larger its self-transition probability should become.

---

# Estimating Emission Distributions

Once observations are assigned to each state,

the model estimates that state's probability distribution.

Suppose State 1 currently contains

```text
13

7

9

7

2

10

1

3

7

8
```

The model computes

$$
\mu
=
\text{Mean}
$$

and

$$
\sigma
=
\text{Standard Deviation}
$$

These define the state's Gaussian emission distribution.

```text
Probability

          /\
         /  \
________/____\________

          μ
```

Each hidden state develops its own statistical description of the observations it usually generates.

---

# Why One Pass Isn't Enough

The initial segmentation is only an approximation.

For example,

suppose one observation was assigned here.

```text
State 1 | State 2

... 5 | 2 ...
```

After estimating the Gaussians,

the model may realize

> Observation **2** actually fits State 1 much better.

The boundary moves.

```text
Before

□□□□|□□□□

↓

After

□□□□□|□□□
```

The model continuously adjusts its understanding of where state boundaries should be.

---

# Learning is an Iterative Process

Training repeatedly alternates between

```text
Estimate Parameters

↓

Reassign Observations

↓

Estimate Parameters

↓

Reassign Observations

↓

...
```

Each iteration makes the model slightly better.

Eventually,

almost no observations change states.

At that point,

training has **converged**.

---

# Viterbi Alignment (Hard Assignment)

The lecture first introduces **Viterbi Alignment**.

Its philosophy is simple.

Every observation belongs to **exactly one state**.

```text
Observation

↓

State 2

100%
```

The model commits to one explanation.

This is called a **hard assignment**.

```text
Observation Sequence

↓

Best State Sequence

↓

Update Parameters
```

Viterbi Alignment is often used to initialize later optimization.

---

# Why Hard Assignment Can Be Limiting

Reality is often uncertain.

Suppose an observation lies exactly between two states.

```text
State 1

██████

State 2

██████
```

Which state should receive it? A hard assignment forces a decision.

Sometimes that decision is wrong. Instead of committing, we may want to express uncertainty.

---

# Baum–Welch (Soft Assignment)

Baum–Welch solves this problem differently. Instead of assigning an observation entirely to one state, it distributes responsibility across **all states**.

Example

```text
Observation = 2

State 1

█████████ 70%

State 2

████ 25%

State 3

█ 5%
```

Every state learns from the observation, but **not equally**.

States that explain it better receive larger updates.

## Hard vs Soft Assignment

| Viterbi Alignment | Baum–Welch |
|-------------------|------------|
| One state receives the observation | Every state receives a weighted contribution |
| Hard assignment | Soft assignment |
| Faster | More statistically accurate |
| Good initialization | Better parameter estimation |

> [!important]
> Viterbi asks:
>
> > **"Which single state is most likely?"**
>
> Baum–Welch asks:
>
> > **"How much should every state take responsibility?"**

---

# Forward–Backward Algorithm

How does Baum–Welch compute these weights efficiently?

It uses the **Forward–Backward Algorithm**.

Unlike Viterbi,

which follows **one best path**,

Forward–Backward considers

**every possible hidden path**.

```text
Possible Paths

Path A

Path B

Path C

Path D

↓

Weighted Together
```

Each path contributes according to its probability.

Observations therefore influence every state proportionally.

---

# Baum–Welch is an EM Algorithm

Baum–Welch belongs to a broader family of optimization algorithms called

**Expectation–Maximization (EM).**

Each iteration contains two phases.

### Expectation (E-Step)

Estimate

> Which hidden states probably generated each observation?

```text
Current Model

↓

Estimate Hidden States
```

---

### Maximization (M-Step)

Update the HMM parameters using those estimates.

```text
Estimated States

↓

Update

Means

Variances

Transitions
```

This repeats until the parameters stabilize.

```text
Current Model

↓

E-Step

↓

M-Step

↓

Improved Model

↓

Repeat
```

---

# The Complete Training Pipeline

```text
Training Examples

        │
        ▼

Initial Segmentation

        │
        ▼

Estimate

Transitions

+

Emissions

        │
        ▼

Viterbi Alignment

(Optional Initialization)

        │
        ▼

Baum–Welch

(E-Step + M-Step)

        │
        ▼

Repeat Until Convergence

        │
        ▼

Final HMM
```

---

# Big Picture

Training an HMM is fundamentally different from recognition.

Recognition asks

> **Which model best explains this sequence?**

Training asks

> **How should the model change so that it explains *all* training sequences better?**

One uses the model.

The other improves it.

---

> [!success]
> Hidden Markov Models solve two complementary problems:
>
> - **Decoding** (Viterbi): infer the most probable hidden state sequence for a given observation sequence.
> - **Learning** (Baum–Welch): adjust the HMM itself so that future observation sequences become more likely under the model.
>
> Together, these algorithms allow HMMs to both **recognize** temporal patterns and **learn** from experience, making them one of the foundational probabilistic models for sequential data before the deep learning era.

> - how Hidden Markov Models represent temporal processes,

> - and how HMM parameters are learned from data.
>

>
> **How do we improve a basic HMM so it works well in real-world recognition systems?**
>
> Modern HMM-based recognizers introduce richer features, better probability models, improved state structures, and more flexible topologies to model complex temporal data.

# Why Improve an HMM?

Early examples in this course intentionally used **very simple models**.

For example, gesture recognition only used

- ΔY (vertical movement)

This made the algorithms easier to understand.

Real recognition systems are much richer.

Instead of describing an observation with **one number**, they often describe it with **dozens of measurements simultaneously.**

> [!important]
> The **recognition algorithm does not change.**
>
> We simply provide the HMM with **better observations** describing the real world.

---

# Multidimensional Observations

## The Problem

Suppose we want to recognize a hand gesture.

Using only

```
ΔY
```

means we only know whether the hand moved

- upward
- downward
- remained still

But many important properties are ignored.

For example

- horizontal movement
- hand orientation
- distance from the camera
- finger configuration
- which hand is moving

Clearly, one feature is insufficient.


## Richer Feature Representation

Instead of describing one frame using a single value,

we describe it using an **observation vector**.

```text
Video Frame

      │

      ▼

Feature Extraction

┌────────────────────────────┐
│ ΔX                         │
│ ΔY                         │
│ Hand Size                  │
│ Hand Orientation           │
│ Left Hand Position         │
│ Right Hand Position        │
│ Finger Angle               │
│ ...                        │
└────────────────────────────┘

      │

      ▼

Observation Vector xₜ
```

Each frame now becomes a point in a **high-dimensional feature space**.


## Example Feature Set

| Feature | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| ΔX | Horizontal movement |
| ΔY | Vertical movement |
| Hand Size | Distance from camera |
| Orientation | Rotation of hand |
| Left-hand ΔX | Left-hand motion |
| Left-hand ΔY | Left-hand motion |
| Right-hand ΔX | Right-hand motion |
| Right-hand ΔY | Right-hand motion |

Instead of

```
Observation = 2
```

we now have

```
Observation

[2, -1, 45°, 18px, ...]
```


> [!tip]
> Modern computer vision systems often represent each frame using **hundreds or even thousands of features**.
>
> The HMM itself remains unchanged—the observations simply become much richer.

---

# Multivariate Gaussian Emissions

Earlier in the lecture,

each state modeled a **single variable** using a Gaussian distribution.

```
State

↓

ΔY
```

Now every observation contains many variables.

Instead of

```
Gaussian(ΔY)
```

we use

```
Multivariate Gaussian

(ΔX, ΔY, Size, Angle, ...)
```

Conceptually,

the probability distribution changes from

```text
One Dimension

      /\
_____/  \_____
```

to

```text
Many Dimensions

        ◉

    Probability Cloud
```

Although visualization becomes difficult,

the mathematics extends naturally.

---

> [!important]
> Training and recognition algorithms remain **exactly the same**.
>
> Only the probability distribution describing each state becomes multidimensional.

---

# When One Gaussian Is Not Enough

A Gaussian assumes observations form **one cluster**.

Unfortunately,

real data often contains **multiple clusters**.

Imagine one HMM state represents

> "Hand near the face."

Different people naturally perform this differently.

Some place their hand

```text
Near left cheek
```

Others place it

```text
Near right cheek
```

The observations therefore form **two groups**.

```text
Cluster A

      /\

_____/  \_____

               Cluster B

                    /\

_______________/  \_____
```

One Gaussian tries to cover both groups.

```text
        ___________

_______/           \_______
```

This is a poor fit.

---

# Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

Instead of using

```
One Gaussian
```

we use

```
Gaussian A

+

Gaussian B
```

This combination is called a

> **Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM).**

Conceptually,

```text
Real Distribution

████████████████████

Approximate using

   /\      /\

__/  \____/  \____
```

Each Gaussian models one portion of the data.

## Why Does This Help?

A mixture allows one state to model

- different speaking styles
- different hand positions
- different handwriting styles
- natural human variation

without requiring extra states.

---

> [!note]
> In practice,
>
> **2–3 Gaussian components**
>
> are often sufficient.
>
> Too many mixtures increase the risk of **overfitting**.

---

# HMM Topology

An HMM is not defined only by its probabilities.

Its **structure** also matters.

Different recognition problems naturally require different state layouts.

```text
Different Tasks

        │

        ▼

Different Temporal Structure

        │

        ▼

Different HMM Topology
```

---

# Example: Speech

Speech naturally progresses

```text
Beginning

↓

Middle

↓

End
```

A simple left-to-right HMM models this well.

```text
S1 → S2 → S3
```

---

# Example: Walking

Walking is different.

The motion repeats.

```text
Left Step

↓

Right Step

↓

Left Step

↓

Right Step
```

A strict left-to-right model would fail.

Instead,

the HMM needs loops.

---

# Looping Topologies

Consider the sign

```
TABLE
```

The movement repeats.

```text
Up

↓

Down

↓

Up

↓

Down
```

Sometimes a signer repeats the movement

while thinking.

```text
Up

↓

Down

↓

Up

↓

Down

↓

Up

↓

Down
```

Rather than building many states,

we allow a loop.

```text
S1 → S2 → S3
      ▲   │
      └───┘
```

Now the middle motion may repeat naturally.

---

> [!important] 
> Loops allow the model to represent **an unknown number of repeated movements** without increasing the number of states.

---

# Choosing the Right Topology

Different gestures require different structures.

Some naturally contain

- three phases

Others contain

- repeated motions

Others may branch into multiple alternatives.

There is no universally best topology.

---

> [!tip] 
> Different HMM structures are usually compared using **cross-validation**.
>
> The topology producing the highest recognition accuracy is selected.

---

# Multiple Models for One Concept

Some words have multiple valid pronunciations.

Likewise,

some sign language words have multiple correct gestures.

Example

```
CAT
```

may be signed

- with one hand
- with two hands

Trying to force both styles into one HMM creates a complicated model.

Instead,

build two independent HMMs.

```text
CAT₁

CAT₂

↓

Recognition

↓

CAT
```

During recognition,

both models compete.

Whichever assigns the higher probability wins.

---

> [!success]
> Multiple simpler HMMs often outperform one overly complicated HMM,
> provided enough training data exists for each variation.

---

# Design Philosophy

Notice the pattern throughout this lecture.

Whenever real-world variability increases,

we **do not replace the HMM.**

Instead,

we enrich one of its components.

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| One feature is insufficient | Add more features |
| One Gaussian is insufficient | Use a Gaussian Mixture |
| One topology is insufficient | Design a better topology |
| One gesture has multiple forms | Train multiple HMMs |

---

> [!summary] Part G Summary
>
> Practical HMM systems become more accurate by improving **what the model represents**, rather than changing the recognition algorithm itself.
>
> Key ideas introduced:
>
> - Rich, multidimensional feature vectors
> - Multivariate Gaussian emissions
> - Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)
> - Flexible HMM topologies
> - Looping states for repeated motions
> - Multiple HMMs representing different variations of the same concept
>
> These improvements form the foundation of large-scale HMM systems used in speech recognition, handwriting recognition, and sign language recognition before the deep learning era.

# Practical Improvements to Hidden Markov Models

> [!abstract]- Overview
> By this point, we already know how an HMM represents a sequence and how the Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely hidden-state path.
>
> This section focuses on **practical improvements** that make HMMs accurate enough for real-world systems such as speech recognition, sign language recognition, and handwriting recognition.
>
> The key idea is that **the core HMM algorithm remains unchanged**—instead, we improve:
>
> - the observations supplied to the model,
> - the probability distributions,
> - the topology of the model,
> - and the search strategy.

---

# The Evolution of an HMM

The progression throughout the lecture can be summarized as

```text
Basic HMM
     │
     ▼
Multiple Features
     │
     ▼
Better Probability Models
     │
     ▼
Better State Structure
     │
     ▼
Better Search
     │
     ▼
Real Recognition System
```

Each improvement addresses a limitation of the previous model rather than replacing it.

---

# 1. Richer Observations

> [!question]
> Is a single feature really enough to describe a gesture?

Usually **no**.

Earlier, the lecture intentionally used only **ΔY** so that the strength of HMMs came from modeling **time** rather than feature engineering.

Real systems instead observe **many properties simultaneously**.

## Example: Sign Language

Instead of representing each frame by a single value

```text
Frame

↓

ΔY
```

each frame becomes a feature vector.

```text
Frame

↓

┌─────────────────────────────┐
│ ΔX │ ΔY │ Size │ Angle │ ... │
└─────────────────────────────┘
```

Possible measurements include

| Feature | Represents |
|----------|------------|
| ΔX | Horizontal motion |
| ΔY | Vertical motion |
| Hand size | Motion toward / away from camera |
| Orientation | Rotation of the hand |
| Left-hand motion | Two-handed gestures |
| Right-hand motion | Two-handed gestures |

---

### Visual intuition

```text
Old observation

Time

● ● ● ●

↓

1 number/frame



New observation

Time

● ● ● ●

↓

8 numbers/frame
```

The HMM still receives **one observation per frame**—that observation simply contains more information.

> [!important]
> The **states**, **transition probabilities**, and **Viterbi algorithm** do **not** change.
>
> Only the observation model becomes multidimensional.

---

# Multivariate Gaussian

Previously every state modeled

```text
ΔY
```

using a one-dimensional Gaussian.

Now each state models

```text
(ΔX, ΔY, Size, Angle, ...)
```

using a **multivariate Gaussian**.

Instead of

$$
p(x)
$$

we estimate

$$
p(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)
$$

where every observation is now a vector.

> [!tip]
> You can think of a multivariate Gaussian as the multidimensional extension of the bell curve you already know.

---

# 2. One Gaussian Isn't Always Enough

Real data is rarely perfectly bell-shaped.

Suppose one HMM state represents a hand movement that can be performed in two equally valid ways.

The observations may look like

```text
          /\      /\
         /  \    /  \
________/    \__/    \____
```

A single Gaussian would place its peak **between** these two clusters—exactly where almost no observations occur.

## Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

Instead of forcing one bell curve,

we combine several.

```text
Gaussian 1

      /\

_____/  \_____



Gaussian 2

             /\

____________/  \_____



↓

Combined

      /\      /\
_____/  \____/  \____
```

The state now represents

```text
Observation Distribution

=

Gaussian 1

+

Gaussian 2

(+ Gaussian 3 ...)
```


> [!success]
> A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) allows a single HMM state to represent **multiple common variations** of the same motion.


## Practical trade-off

Too few Gaussians

```text
Poor fit
```

Too many

```text
Fits noise
↓

Overfitting
```

The lecture notes that **2–3 Gaussians** are often sufficient.

---

# Why GMMs Help

Imagine two people performing the same sign.

```text
Person A

Hand slightly left



Person B

Hand slightly right
```

Both are correct.

Rather than creating separate HMM states,

a mixture model captures both variations naturally.

---

# Key Idea

> [!important]
> Increasing the number of Gaussians does **not** create new states.
>
> It simply allows **one state** to explain a wider variety of observations.

---

# Engineering Perspective

Each improvement we've seen follows the same pattern:

| Improvement | What changes? | What stays the same? |
|--------------|---------------|----------------------|
| More features | Observation vector | HMM algorithm |
| Multivariate Gaussian | Emission probability | Viterbi |
| Gaussian Mixture Model | Observation distribution | State transitions |

Notice that none of these modifications alter the **core HMM framework**—they simply make its probability estimates more realistic.

# Takeaways

> [!summary]
> - A single feature rarely captures enough information for real recognition tasks.
> - Real systems describe each frame using **feature vectors** rather than individual values.
> - One Gaussian may be too simple to represent complex observations.
> - **Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)** allow each state to model several common patterns.
> - These improvements enhance the observation model while leaving the HMM algorithm itself unchanged.

## Big Picture: Pattern Recognition Through Time

> [!abstract] From Sequence Comparison to Sequence Modeling
> This lecture introduced two major approaches for working with temporal data:
>
> - **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)** compares **two sequences** by finding the best alignment.
> - **Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)** learn a **probabilistic model** capable of recognizing an entire class of sequences.
>
> Together, they illustrate the evolution from simple sequence comparison to probabilistic sequence modeling.

```text
                    Pattern Recognition Through Time
                                 │
                                 ▼
                     Temporal / Sequential Data
                                 │
          ┌──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┐
          │                                                                                            │
          ▼                                                                                            ▼
   Compare Sequences                                                          Model Sequences
          │                                                                                            │
          ▼                                                                                            ▼
 Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)                                   Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)
          │                                                                                            │
          ▼                                                                                            ▼
 Flexible Time Alignment                                             Hidden States + Observations
          │                                                                                            │
          ▼                                                                                            ▼
      Similarity Score                                                 Most Probable State Sequence
                                                    │
                                                    ▼
                                               Viterbi Algorithm
                                                    │
                                                    ▼
                                                HMM Training
                                                    │
                           ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
                           ▼                                                                                                    ▼
                  Viterbi Alignment                                                                            Baum–Welch (EM)
                                                    │
                                                    ▼
                                       Practical Recognition Systems
                                                    │
                                                    ▼
                      GMMs • Context Training • Beam Search • Grammar
```

> [!tip]
> A useful way to remember the distinction:
>
> - **DTW asks:** *"How similar are these two sequences?"*
> - **HMM asks:** *"How likely is this sequence to have been generated by this model?"*

## DTW vs HMM vs Euclidean Distance

| Method | Learns from Data? | Handles Variable Speed? | Models Hidden Process? | Primary Purpose |
|----------|:----------------:|:----------------------:|:----------------------:|-----------------|
| **Euclidean Distance** | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | Compare fixed-length vectors |
| **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)** | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | Compare two temporal sequences by aligning them |
| **Hidden Markov Model (HMM)** | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | Learn and recognize classes of temporal sequences |

### Interpretation

> [!example]
> Suppose two people say the word **"hello."**
>
> **Euclidean Distance**
>
> - compares samples one-to-one
> - fails if one person speaks more slowly
>
> **Dynamic Time Warping**
>
> - stretches or compresses time
> - successfully aligns both pronunciations
>
> **Hidden Markov Model**
>
> - does not compare the recordings directly
> - instead asks:
>
> > *"How likely is each recording to have been generated by my learned model of the word 'hello'?"*

> [!success]
> Think of the progression as:
>
> ```text
> Euclidean Distance
>          │
>          ▼
> Better comparison
>          │
>          ▼
> Dynamic Time Warping
>          │
>          ▼
> Better modeling
>          │
>          ▼
> Hidden Markov Models
> ```

## Historical Perspective: From HMMs to Deep Learning

> [!insight]
> Hidden Markov Models dominated sequence recognition for nearly three decades and formed the foundation of many commercial speech and handwriting recognition systems.
>
> Although modern AI often relies on deep learning, many of the ideas introduced by HMMs remain fundamental.

### Evolution of Sequence Models

```text
1980s–1990s

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)

↓

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)

↓

HMM + Gaussian Mixture Models

↓

HMM + Statistical Language Models

↓

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)

↓

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)

↓

LSTMs / GRUs

↓

Transformers
```

### Why Did Deep Learning Replace HMMs?

Traditional HMM pipelines required extensive manual engineering:

- carefully designed feature vectors
- manually selected HMM topologies
- Gaussian mixture tuning
- context-dependent models
- statistical grammars

Modern deep learning models learn many of these representations automatically from large datasets.

### What Ideas Remain Important?

Although newer models have replaced HMMs in many applications, the underlying concepts remain highly relevant.

| Concept | Still Important Today? |
|----------|:----------------------:|
| Sequential modeling | ✅ |
| Hidden latent representations | ✅ |
| Probabilistic reasoning | ✅ |
| Dynamic programming for inference | ✅ |
| Language constraints | ✅ |
| Temporal dependencies | ✅ |

> [!important]
> Modern sequence models (such as **LSTMs** and **Transformers**) are not replacements for the ideas introduced by HMMs—they are more powerful methods for solving the same fundamental problem:
>
> **Understanding data whose meaning depends on time and order.**
